# Tail-Latency Amplification in Fan-Out LLM Workloads
researched: 2026-08-27 · researcher: glm-5.3-flash

## Key facts
- Dean & Barroso's "The Tail at Scale" (CACM 56(2), 2013) is the lineage paper: in large services, an individual machine can be slow on ~1% of requests, yet a request touching many machines sees the *maximum* of many latency draws, so "temporary high latency episodes which are unimportant in moderate size systems may come to dominate overall service performance at large scale." (Dean & Barroso, CACM 2013)
- Core derived math: if each call exceeds latency L with probability p, a fan-out of N independent calls exceeds L with probability 1−(1−p)^N (derived). At p = 1% and N = 100: 1−0.99^100 ≈ 63% (derived). At N = 10,000 (a "10k-call fanout" batch-eval or map step): 1−0.99^10000 ≈ 99.99999% — a once-in-a-hundred tail event becomes essentially every step (derived).
- Consequence (derived): a step's latency percentile approaches the max percentile of its children. With N = 100 p50-median calls, the step's latency is governed by roughly the ~99th percentile of the per-call distribution (derived); with N = 10,000, by roughly the ~99.99th percentile (derived) — assuming independence, which real serving clusters only loosely satisfy (shared batch-scheduling queues correlate tails).
- The paper's canonical hedging example: hedging a BigTable read with a second replica request delayed to the 95th percentile latency reduced 99.9th-percentile latency from 1,800 ms to 74 ms (Dean & Barroso, CACM 2013).
- The same paper lists per-request timeouts, hedged requests, "tardigrade"-style fast replicas, and cross-request variance control as the standard mitigation toolkit; it explicitly frames this as building a "predictably responsive whole out of less predictable parts" (research.google abstract, fetched 2026-08-27).
- LLM serving reintroduces exactly the variance sources Dean & Barroso describe: prefill–decode interference. DistServe (OSDI 2024) shows colocated prefill/decode batching causes "strong prefill-decoding interferences," and that disaggregation lets it serve 7.4× more requests or meet a 12.6× tighter latency SLO while keeping >90% of requests within TTFT/TPOT constraints (arXiv:2401.09670, 2024).
- Sarathi-Serve shows the tail-throughput coupling quantitatively: chunked-prefill, stall-free scheduling yields 2.6× higher serving capacity for Mistral-7B (1×A100) and up to 5.6× for Falcon-180B with pipeline parallelism *under tail-latency (TTFT/TPOT) constraints*, versus vLLM (arXiv:2403.02310, 2024). Translation: at high utilization, tail latency — not the median — caps how much fan-out a cluster absorbs.
- OpenAI's own latency guide recommends parallelism for non-sequential steps ("two shirts take just as long to dry as one") and speculative execution, but also warns that "each time you make a request, you incur some round-trip latency — this can start to add up," and ranks "make fewer requests" alongside "parallelize" (OpenAI platform docs, latency optimization guide, fetched 2026-08-27).
- Token-side lever, same docs: cutting ~50% of output tokens may cut ~50% of latency, while cutting ~50% of prompt tokens often yields only a 1–5% latency improvement (OpenAI platform docs, fetched 2026-08-27). This matters for fan-outs because max-over-N is driven by the *slowest* child, which is usually the child that emitted the most output tokens.
- No major provider publishes p99 TTFT/TPOT on a status page that this researcher could verify today; treat any third-party "provider p99" numbers as community benchmarks, approximate (unverified as of 2026-08-27). Use provider status pages (status.openai.com, status.anthropic.com) for incident-derived tail evidence, not percentile distributions.

## How it works
A fan-out step — a map-reduce agent scoring N candidates in parallel — does not finish at the average of N latencies; it finishes at the maximum. Each child call is a draw from the provider's latency distribution, and the step's completion time is the worst draw. Order statistics are unforgiving: the expected maximum of N draws from a distribution sits at roughly the (1−1/N) quantile of that distribution. So a 100-wide fan-out of perfectly median (p50) calls behaves, at the step level, like a p99 call; a 10,000-wide batch-eval fan-out behaves like a p99.99 call. If the per-call distribution has a heavy right tail — and LLM serving tails are heavy — the step latency can be an order of magnitude worse than the typical call, and worse than any single call you would ever have noticed in testing.

LLM engines make the tail heavier than classic web services for structural reasons. A single request alternates phases (prefill then decode), and serving engines batch across requests; one request's giant prompt prefill stalls everyone else's decode ticks in the same batch, so one outlier child's input size becomes every sibling's latency. DistServe (OSDI 2024) measured this interference and moved prefill and decode to different GPUs precisely to decouple the tails; Sarathi-Serve (2024) attacks the same problem with chunked prefills so new requests join batches without pausing ongoing decodes. From the client side you cannot see any of this — you just see that your per-call p99 drifted from 800 ms to 8 s during someone else's 50k-token batch job, and your fan-out step waits for it.

The classic mitigations transfer, with twists. Hedged requests (issue a duplicate to a different replica/provider after a delay near the per-call p95) work because tail events are largely uncorrelated across replicas — but with LLMs each hedge costs real tokens/money, and non-determinism means the two replies can differ. Per-call timeouts must be at the *child* level, not the HTTP level, and they interact with output length: a timeout that kills the longest-output child biases your reduce step toward short answers. Deadline propagation means the fan-out step owns a budget (e.g., 3 s) and children are launched with `min(budget_remaining, child_p95)`; late children are either retried once hedged or simply dropped under partial-result acceptance, where the reduce step is designed to degrade gracefully with K-of-N results (e.g., score the top 90% of candidates returned).

## Harness angle
A harness that runs parallel tool/model calls must ship fan-out as a first-class primitive with a deadline, not as N bare API calls: budget the step, give every child its own timeout derived from a live per-model p95 estimate, hedge only the stragglers past p95, and let the reduce step declare a completion policy (all / K-of-N / first-result) so one slow provider draw cannot define the step's latency. Concretely: never design a map step whose correctness requires all N results.
